FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Trade finance is a broad term covering various financial products that facilitate international and domestic trade. In its simplest form, a trade finance facility pays your suppliers on your behalf, and you repay the lender after an agreed period (typically 30–180 days). This means you can receive and sell goods before you need to pay for them, preserving your working capital. Products include import finance, export finance, letters of credit, documentary collections, and supply chain finance.
A letter of credit is a guarantee from your bank to your supplier’s bank that payment will be made once specific shipping conditions are met (e.g., goods shipped, documents presented). You typically need an LC when trading with new suppliers, dealing in high-value transactions, or when your supplier requires payment security. LCs add cost (0.5%–2% of the LC value) but significantly reduce risk for both parties.
Trade finance is typically much cheaper than business loans because the facility is secured by the goods being traded and the transaction itself. Rates typically range from 1.5% to 5% p.a. compared to 6%–25% for business loans. However, trade finance facilities are short-term (30–180 days) and transaction-specific, so they’re not a replacement for long-term funding needs.
Import finance funds the purchase of goods from overseas suppliers. The lender pays your supplier and you repay after an agreed period. Export finance funds the gap between shipping goods to an international buyer and receiving payment. Both are forms of trade finance, but they serve different sides of the trade transaction and may have different eligibility criteria and documentation requirements.
Currency risk is a significant consideration for importers and exporters. Most trade finance lenders offer multi-currency facilities, and many also provide foreign exchange (FX) hedging products. Forward contracts allow you to lock in an exchange rate for a future date, eliminating uncertainty. We recommend discussing FX hedging as part of your trade finance application.
It’s more challenging but not impossible. Most major bank trade finance facilities require 12+ months of trading history. However, some specialist trade financiers will consider newer businesses if you have strong personal financials, a solid business plan, and confirmed purchase orders. Your Compare Report will identify which lenders are willing to work with newer importers.
Trade finance is often secured by the goods being traded (the inventory itself serves as collateral) and the supporting trade documents (bills of lading, invoices, etc.). Some lenders may also require a general security agreement (GSA) over your business assets, personal guarantees from directors, or in some cases, property security.
Initial setup typically takes 7–21 business days from application to approval, depending on the lender and complexity of your trade. Major banks tend to take 14–21 days, while non-bank lenders can often approve in 7–14 days. Once a facility is established, individual drawdowns are typically processed within 1–3 business days.
Supply chain finance (SCF) is a broader approach that optimises cash flow across your entire supply chain. Unlike traditional trade finance which focuses on individual transactions, SCF programs allow your suppliers to be paid early (at a discount) while you extend your payment terms. It’s most effective for larger businesses with established supply chains and significant purchasing volumes.
Trade finance is one of the most complex areas of business lending. Each lender has different products, credit appetite, and pricing structures. Going direct means you only see one offer.
