Frequently asked questions.

What is the difference between an operating lease and a finance lease?

The fundamental difference is ownership intent. With an operating lease, you use the equipment for a set term and then return it, upgrade, or purchase at fair market value — there is no automatic transfer of ownership. With a finance lease, the intention from the outset is for you to own the asset at the end of the term by paying a residual. Operating leases typically have lower monthly payments because you're only paying for the use of the asset, not its full value.

How does AASB 16 affect operating leases?

AASB 16 changed the accounting treatment for operating leases for many businesses. Under the previous standard, operating leases sat off balance sheet, with only the lease payments appearing as an expense. Under AASB 16, most leases must now be recognised on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a corresponding lease liability, though exemptions exist for short-term leases of 12 months or less, low-value assets, and small businesses using simplified reporting. Speak with your accountant about how it applies to you.

What types of assets are best suited to an operating lease?

Operating leases suit assets that depreciate quickly or become obsolete, where you want to upgrade regularly. Common examples include IT equipment, vehicles and fleet, office equipment, medical equipment, and point-of-sale systems. The key criteria: the asset loses value fast, you don't need long-term ownership, and you want the flexibility to move to newer equipment at the end of the term.

What happens at the end of an operating lease?

You typically have three options: return the asset to the lessor, which is the most common choice for technology assets; upgrade to new equipment and start a fresh lease; or, with some agreements, purchase the asset at its fair market value rather than a nominal residual. The options available depend on your specific lease agreement.
