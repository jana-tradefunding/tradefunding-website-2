Invoice Finance vs Debtor Finance: Which Is Right for Your Business?

Published 23 March 2026 9 min read

If your business invoices other businesses for goods or services, you have probably heard the terms invoice finance, debtor finance, and factoring thrown around, often interchangeably. While they all involve using your unpaid invoices to access cash flow, they are actually distinct products with different structures, costs, and implications for your business.

Choosing the wrong product can mean paying thousands more than necessary, being locked into an inflexible contract, or even damaging customer relationships. This guide breaks down the key differences so you can make an informed decision about which type of receivables financing suits your business best in 2026.

Definitions: What Each Term Actually Means

Invoice Finance (Selective or Single Invoice)

Invoice finance in its simplest form means borrowing against a specific invoice or a selection of invoices that you choose. A lender advances you typically 80–90% of the invoice value upfront. When your customer pays the invoice, you receive the remaining balance minus the lender's fees.

The key feature is selectivity. You decide which invoices to finance and when. You are not required to commit your entire debtor book, and there is typically no long-term contract. Platforms like FundThat and Waddle allow you to upload individual invoices and receive funds within 24–48 hours.

Debtor Finance (Whole-of-Book Factoring)

Debtor finance, also commonly called factoring, is a broader facility where you assign your entire debtor book (or a substantial portion of it) to a finance provider. The lender provides an ongoing credit line based on the total value of your outstanding receivables. As you raise new invoices, they are automatically included in the facility. As customers pay, your available funding replenishes.

Major debtor finance providers in Australia include ScotPac, Earlypay, Octet, and the debtor finance divisions of most major banks. Facilities typically start at $100,000 and can scale into the tens of millions for larger businesses.

Factoring vs Invoice Discounting

Within debtor finance, there are two important sub-types:

  • Factoring (disclosed): The finance provider takes over the collection process. They chase your customers for payment directly. Your customers know a third party is involved. Best for businesses that want to outsource credit management and collections.
  • Invoice discounting (confidential): You continue to collect payments from customers as normal. The facility can be confidential, meaning your customers never know you are using finance. Best for businesses that want to maintain direct customer relationships.

How Invoice Finance Works: A Practical Example

Say you are a digital marketing agency that has just completed a $50,000 project for a large corporate client. The invoice is due in 45 days, but you need to pay your contractors and cover next month's office rent.

  1. You upload the $50,000 invoice to a selective invoice finance platform.
  2. The lender verifies the invoice and your client's creditworthiness (usually within hours).
  3. Within 24–48 hours, you receive an advance of $42,500 (85% of the invoice value).
  4. Your client pays the full $50,000 invoice after 45 days.
  5. The lender deducts their fee (say 2.5%, or $1,250) and remits the remaining $6,250 to you.

Total cost: $1,250 to access $42,500 six weeks early. For most businesses, this is a worthwhile trade-off compared to missing payroll, turning down new work, or taking on more expensive short-term debt.

How Debtor Finance Works: A Practical Example

Now consider a wholesale distributor turning over $3 million per year with a consistent debtor book of $400,000. Their customers typically pay on 30–60 day terms, and the business needs ongoing working capital to purchase stock.

  1. The distributor sets up a whole-of-book debtor finance facility with a provider like ScotPac or Earlypay.
  2. The lender assesses the debtor book and offers an advance rate of 85%, providing access to up to $340,000 in available funding.
  3. As new invoices are raised, they are automatically included in the facility. As customers pay, the available pool replenishes.
  4. The distributor draws down and repays as needed, paying a discount rate of approximately 10% p.a. on amounts drawn, plus a service fee of 0.75% of turnover processed.

This ongoing facility acts like a revolving credit line secured against the debtor book, providing consistent working capital without the need to apply for new funding every time cash gets tight.

Cost Comparison: Which Is Cheaper?

The answer depends entirely on how frequently you need to finance invoices and the total volume.

If you only finance invoices a few times per year, selective invoice finance is almost always cheaper. If you need consistent, ongoing access to working capital, a whole-of-book debtor finance facility often works out more cost-effective per dollar advanced.

For businesses financing over $1 million per year in invoices, whole-of-book facilities generally become the more economical option on a per-dollar basis.

When to Use Invoice Finance

  • You have a few large invoices that you need to convert to cash quickly
  • You do not want to be locked into a long-term contract
  • Your business is relatively new (under 12 months) and may not qualify for a full facility
  • You want to maintain full control over customer relationships and collections
  • You have a handful of slow-paying customers but most pay on time
  • You need funding on a project-by-project basis rather than continuously

When to Use Debtor Finance

  • You have a large, diversified debtor book worth $100,000 or more
  • You need consistent, ongoing working capital rather than occasional funding
  • You are growing rapidly and your funding needs scale with revenue
  • You want to outsource credit management and debtor collections (factoring model)
  • You are comfortable with a 12–24 month contract commitment
  • Your business invoices many different customers rather than relying on a few key accounts

Recourse vs Non-Recourse: Understanding the Risk

One critical distinction that applies to both invoice finance and debtor finance is whether the facility is recourse or non-recourse:

Recourse: If your customer fails to pay the invoice, the debt reverts back to you. You must repay the advance. This is the most common arrangement in Australia and carries lower fees because the lender's risk is reduced.

Non-recourse: If your customer fails to pay (due to insolvency, for example), the lender absorbs the loss. You are not required to repay the advance. This provides protection against bad debts but typically costs 0.5–1% more in fees. Non-recourse is more common with debtor finance facilities but is available on some selective platforms too.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between invoice finance and debtor finance?

Invoice finance typically refers to financing individual invoices on a selective basis, while debtor finance (also called factoring) usually involves assigning your entire debtor book or a substantial portion of it to the lender. Invoice finance gives you more flexibility to choose which invoices to fund, while debtor finance provides more comprehensive ongoing working capital.

How much does invoice finance cost in Australia?

Costs typically range from 1–3% per invoice for single invoice finance, or a discount rate of 8–15% p.a. for whole-of-book facilities. Additional fees may include service fees (0.5–1.5% of invoice value), establishment fees, and minimum monthly charges. The total cost depends on invoice size, payment terms, and debtor quality.

Do my customers know I am using invoice finance?

It depends on the type. Disclosed (notified) facilities mean your customers are told payments should go to the finance provider. Confidential (undisclosed) facilities allow you to collect payments as normal. Confidential facilities typically cost slightly more but preserve your customer relationships.

What happens if my customer does not pay the invoice?

With recourse finance (the most common type in Australia), the debt reverts back to you and you must repay the advance. With non-recourse finance, the lender absorbs the bad debt risk, but this costs more, typically an additional 0.5–1% on the discount rate.

What size business can use invoice finance?

Invoice finance is available for businesses of almost any size. Single invoice platforms accept invoices from $5,000 upward. Whole-of-book debtor finance facilities typically require minimum annual revenue of $500,000–$1 million and a debtor book of at least $100,000.
