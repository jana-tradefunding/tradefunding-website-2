Business Line of Credit in Australia: The Complete Guide

Published 5 April 2026 16 min read By Trade Funding

A business line of credit is one of the most flexible forms of commercial finance available to Australian businesses. Unlike a term loan where you receive a lump sum and repay it on a fixed schedule, a line of credit gives you access to a pool of funds that you can draw on whenever you need them and repay at your own pace. You only pay interest on what you actually use.

For businesses that experience fluctuating cash flow, whether from seasonal demand, lumpy revenue, or variable supplier payment terms, a line of credit can be the difference between thriving and scrambling. This guide covers everything you need to know about business lines of credit in Australia in 2026, including how they work, what they cost, who offers them, and how to secure the best facility for your business.

What Is a Business Line of Credit?

A business line of credit is a revolving finance facility with a pre-approved limit. Think of it as a reservoir of capital that you can tap into whenever your business needs it. You draw funds when required, repay them when you can, and the credit becomes available again, a continuous cycle of draw, repay, and redraw.

The key distinction from a term loan is that you are not locked into a fixed repayment schedule on the full amount. If your approved limit is $200,000 but you only need $50,000 this month, you draw $50,000 and pay interest only on that amount. Next month, you might draw another $30,000 or repay the original $50,000. The facility adjusts to your needs.

How a Line of Credit Works in Practice

Here is a practical example. Imagine you run a wholesale distribution business with a $300,000 line of credit:

  1. January: You draw $120,000 to purchase stock for a large order. Interest accrues on $120,000.
  2. February: Your customer pays their invoice. You repay $100,000. Interest now accrues on the remaining $20,000.
  3. March: Quiet month. You draw nothing additional. You repay the remaining $20,000. Interest drops to zero.
  4. April: A new opportunity arises. You draw $80,000. Interest accrues on $80,000.

Throughout this cycle, you had access to up to $300,000 at any time, but you only paid interest on the actual amounts drawn. Compare this to a $300,000 term loan where you would pay interest on the full amount from day one, regardless of whether you needed it all immediately.

Key Advantage: Pay Only for What You Use

The primary benefit of a line of credit is cost efficiency. If your funding needs are irregular or unpredictable, you could pay significantly less in interest than with a term loan, because you are only charged on drawn funds. For businesses with seasonal patterns, this can save thousands of dollars annually.

Types of Business Lines of Credit in Australia

Secured vs Unsecured Lines of Credit

Secured lines of credit are backed by collateral, typically property, but also equipment, inventory, or a General Security Agreement (GSA) over business assets registered on the PPSR. Secured facilities offer lower interest rates (from 8% p.a.), higher credit limits (up to $5 million or more), and longer facility terms. The downside is that your assets are at risk if you default, and the approval process is longer due to valuations and legal documentation.

Unsecured lines of credit require no specific collateral, though most lenders still require a director's personal guarantee. They are faster to establish, involve less paperwork, and are available from a wider range of lenders including fintech providers. The trade-off is higher interest rates (14% to 30% p.a.) and lower credit limits, typically capped at $250,000 to $500,000.

Revolving vs Non-Revolving Facilities

Revolving lines of credit allow you to continuously draw, repay, and redraw up to your limit for the life of the facility. This is the most common type and the one most people think of when they hear "line of credit." The facility typically has an annual review but no fixed end date. As long as you meet the lender's conditions, it continues indefinitely.

Non-revolving (or reducing) lines of credit give you a set period to draw funds, after which the facility converts to a fixed repayment schedule. These are less common but are sometimes used for project-based finance where the borrower needs flexible access during a defined period.

Bank vs Non-Bank Lines of Credit

Major bank facilities (CBA, NAB, ANZ, Westpac) offer the most competitive rates and highest limits, but come with stricter eligibility criteria. Expect to provide 2+ years of financial statements, demonstrate strong profitability, and potentially offer property security. Approval typically takes 10 to 20 business days.

Second-tier banks (Judo Bank, BOQ, Bendigo) fill the gap between major banks and fintech lenders. They offer competitive rates with more flexible eligibility, and many specialise in SME lending. Judo Bank in particular has built its business on serving businesses that the Big 4 overlook.

Fintech and online lenders (Prospa, OnDeck, Moula, Lumi) offer the fastest access. Many can approve and establish a facility within 24 to 48 hours. Eligibility requirements are less stringent (6+ months in business, $100,000+ annual revenue). Rates are higher, but for businesses that cannot access bank credit or need funds urgently, they fill a critical gap.

Fee Types to Watch For

Beyond the interest rate, business lines of credit can carry several fees that affect the total cost:

  • Establishment fee: A one-off fee to set up the facility, typically 0.5% to 2% of the approved limit. On a $300,000 facility, a 1.5% establishment fee adds $4,500.
  • Annual facility fee: Some lenders charge an annual fee to maintain the facility, regardless of whether you use it. This can range from $250 to 1% of the facility limit per year.
  • Unused line fee: A charge on the undrawn portion of your facility, typically 0.5% to 1.5% p.a. If you have a $200,000 limit and only draw $50,000, you might pay 1% on the unused $150,000, an additional $1,500 per year.
  • Draw-down fee: Less common, but some lenders charge a small fee each time you draw funds. Usually $10 to $50 per draw.
  • Review fee: Charged at the annual review of your facility, typically $200 to $500. Some lenders waive this if your account is in good standing.
Watch the Unused Line Fee

The unused line fee is one of the most overlooked costs. If you establish a large facility "just in case" but rarely draw on it, the unused line fee can erode the value of having the facility. Size your facility based on realistic usage, not worst-case scenarios. You can always request a limit increase later.

Credit Limits: How Much Can You Access?

Your approved credit limit depends on multiple factors:

  • Annual revenue: Most lenders will approve a line of credit equal to 10% to 30% of your annual revenue for unsecured facilities. Secured facilities can be significantly higher.
  • Cash flow strength: Lenders assess your monthly cash flow to determine how much you can comfortably draw and repay. Consistent, predictable cash flow supports higher limits.
  • Security offered: Property-secured facilities can reach $5 million or more. Facilities secured by a GSA over business assets typically cap at $1 million to $2 million. Unsecured facilities rarely exceed $500,000.
  • Time in business: Longer trading history generally supports higher limits. Banks want 2+ years for larger facilities.
  • Existing debt: Any current loans, leases, or other credit facilities reduce your available borrowing capacity.

Pros and Cons of a Business Line of Credit

Advantages

  • Pay interest only on what you draw, not the total facility
  • Funds available on demand once established, no reapplication needed
  • Revolving nature means you can use it again and again
  • Flexible repayments, pay down as cash flow allows
  • Useful safety net for unexpected opportunities or costs
  • Less expensive than a term loan for variable or short-term needs
  • Can strengthen supplier relationships by enabling early payment

Disadvantages

  • Variable rates mean interest costs can increase unexpectedly
  • Annual reviews mean the facility can be reduced or cancelled
  • Unused line fees add cost even when you are not drawing
  • Can encourage over-reliance on debt if not managed carefully
  • Unsecured rates (14%+) are significantly higher than term loan bank rates
  • Facility limits are lower than equivalent term loan amounts
  • Some lenders can call in the facility with short notice during reviews

When a Line of Credit Is the Best Choice

  • Seasonal businesses: Retailers, tourism operators, and agricultural businesses with predictable peaks and troughs can draw during slow periods and repay during high-revenue months.
  • Project-based businesses: Construction, consulting, and events companies that need capital for each new project but generate lumpy revenue.
  • Inventory-dependent businesses: Wholesalers and distributors who need to purchase stock in advance of sales.
  • Businesses wanting a safety net: Having an established line of credit means you can act quickly when unexpected opportunities or expenses arise, without going through a full loan application.

When to Choose Something Else

  • If you need a specific amount for a defined purchase (equipment, vehicle, property), a term loan provides certainty and often a lower rate.
  • If you just need a small day-to-day buffer, an overdraft is simpler and attaches to your existing account.
  • If your cash flow issues are driven by slow-paying customers, invoice finance or debtor finance addresses the root cause.

Best Use Cases for a Business Line of Credit

1. Managing Seasonal Cash Flow

A surfwear retailer in Byron Bay generates 60% of its annual revenue between October and February. During the quieter winter months, a $150,000 line of credit covers rent, wages, and stock purchases. The retailer draws $40,000 to $80,000 during the off-season and repays it in full during the summer rush. Total interest cost for the year: approximately $3,200, far less than a 12-month term loan on the same amount.

2. Bridging Supplier Payment Gaps

A Melbourne-based food wholesaler buys from overseas suppliers on 30-day payment terms but sells to restaurants on 60-day terms. The 30-day gap means cash is always tied up. A $250,000 line of credit bridges this gap, ensuring suppliers are paid on time while waiting for restaurant payments to clear.

3. Seizing Time-Sensitive Opportunities

An IT reseller is offered a bulk-buy discount from a vendor: 25% off if they commit to a $180,000 order within 48 hours. Their line of credit lets them say yes immediately, purchase the stock, and repay the draw-down as they sell through the inventory. The discount far exceeds the interest cost of the short-term draw.

4. Managing Payroll During Growth

A recruitment agency is growing fast. Headcount has doubled in 12 months. Revenue is increasing but lags behind payroll commitments because clients pay on 45-day terms. A $200,000 line of credit ensures staff are paid fortnightly while accounts receivable catches up.

Tips for Managing Your Line of Credit Effectively

  1. Treat it as working capital, not a permanent loan. The best use of a line of credit is cyclical: draw, use, repay. If you are constantly at or near your limit without reducing the balance, you may need a term loan instead.
  2. Monitor your utilisation rate. Lenders review your facility annually. Consistently drawing 90%+ of your limit signals that you may be over-leveraged. Conversely, never using the facility suggests you do not need it (and you are paying unused fees for nothing).
  3. Set internal draw-down rules. Decide in advance what the facility is for. Mixing working capital with ad-hoc personal expenses or non-essential spending makes it harder to manage and raises red flags at review time.
  4. Plan for the annual review. Banks reassess your facility every 12 months. Have your latest financials ready, ensure your account is in good standing, and address any concerns proactively. A strong review can lead to a limit increase; a weak one can result in a reduction or cancellation.
  5. Compare your facility every 1 to 2 years. Business lines of credit are not "set and forget." Your business profile changes over time, and better options may become available. Use Trade Funding's Compare Report to check whether your current facility is still competitive.
