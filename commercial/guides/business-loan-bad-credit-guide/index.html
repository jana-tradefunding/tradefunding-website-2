What Counts as "Bad Credit" for a Business Lender?

Lenders don't treat every credit blemish the same way. A single late payment cleared within days reads very differently to an unpaid court judgment or a history of insolvency — and the panel of lenders willing to consider your application narrows in stages, not all at once. Understanding which tier your situation falls into is the first step to knowing which lenders are actually worth approaching.

Most credit files that get labelled "bad credit" fall into one of four severity bands. The further down the list, the smaller the panel, and the more the lender will weight security and current trading performance over the file itself.

Late Payments
Isolated late payments on trade accounts or credit cards, resolved within 30–60 days. Most fintech and non-bank lenders look past these if recent trading is clean.
Defaults
A registered default (unpaid debt over $150 reported to a credit bureau) is the most common reason a business ends up on this page. Age, size, and whether it's paid or unpaid all affect how lenders treat it.
Court Judgments
An unpaid judgment or writ signals the creditor had to escalate to enforcement. This shrinks the panel to specialist non-bank and private lenders, usually with a security requirement attached.
Bankruptcy / Insolvency History
A director's personal bankruptcy, or a prior company liquidation or administration, is the most serious tier. A small number of private lenders will still consider it, generally against strong security and a clear explanation of what changed.

Which Lenders Will Actually Consider You

The bank tier is largely off the table once a default or worse is on file. But "bad credit" doesn't mean no lender — it means a smaller, more specialised panel, priced to reflect the higher risk they're carrying.

What a Default Actually Does to Your Rate

The clearest way to understand the cost of a poor credit file is side by side, on an identical loan. Worked example: $120,000 term loan, 24-month term, both businesses trading profitably with comparable cash flow — the only difference is the credit file.

LENDER A — CLEAN FILE
11.5% p.a.
Origination fee: 2% ($2,400)
Security: Unsecured
Total interest & fees: $32,000
LENDER B — 2-YEAR-OLD DEFAULT
27% p.a.
Origination fee: 3% ($3,600)
Security: Equipment asset offered
Total interest & fees: $79,000

The default adds roughly $47,000 to the cost of the same loan over two years — not because the lender is being punitive, but because it's pricing genuinely higher risk. This is exactly why aging a default out, or offering security to offset it, is worth the wait where the timeline allows it.

Steps to Improve Your Position Before Applying

Where timing allows, a few months of preparation can meaningfully change the panel of lenders available to you, and the rate they offer. None of these are quick fixes, but each one moves the file in the right direction.

Pay Out Small Defaults
A paid default still shows on file, but lenders read it very differently to an unpaid one — it's evidence the debt was resolved, not abandoned.
Let a Default Age
Most defaults stop weighing as heavily on a lending decision once they're past 2 years old, even before they drop off the file entirely at 5 years.
Offer Security
Putting equipment, property, or a debtor book behind the loan directly offsets credit risk in the lender's assessment — often the single biggest lever available.
Build 6 Months of Clean Trading
Recent, stable bank statements are the strongest counter-evidence to an old credit issue. Lenders weight current behaviour heavily.

Real-World Case Study

REGIONAL QUEENSLAND MANUFACTURING

A Toowoomba-based metal fabrication business picked up a $14,000 default in 2023 after a dispute with a materials supplier over a delivery it considered faulty — the debt was ultimately paid, but not before it was reported. By 2026, with the default paid and aged past two years, and 8 months of clean, growing trading behind them, the business needed $95,000 to purchase a second CNC machine. A specialist non-bank lender approved the facility secured against the new equipment itself, at 14.8% p.a. over a 4-year term — well inside the non-bank band despite the default still showing on file, because the security and the recent trading history did the work the credit file couldn't.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a business loan with a default on my file?

Yes, in most cases. Specialist non-bank and private lenders regularly approve applications with a default on file, particularly where the default is paid, aged, or accompanied by security or strong recent trading. The bank tier is unlikely to consider it, but that's a small part of the overall lending market.

Does a personal bankruptcy rule out business finance?

Not entirely, but it's the most serious tier and narrows the panel to a small number of private lenders. Expect a security requirement and a rate at the higher end of the range, along with closer scrutiny of what's changed since the bankruptcy.

How much will bad credit increase my rate?

It varies with severity, age, and whether you can offer security, but the gap is real — often the difference between a rate in the low teens and one in the high twenties or beyond for an otherwise identical loan. Offering security is the most reliable way to close that gap.

Will applying show up on my credit file even if I'm declined?

A formal application typically triggers a hard inquiry regardless of outcome, which is why comparing lenders through a soft check first — before submitting a formal application anywhere — protects a file that may already be under pressure.

Can I get finance with no credit history at all (new business)?

Yes — a thin or absent credit file is treated differently to a poor one. Some fintech and non-bank lenders will assess a new business primarily on the director's personal credit history and the business's early trading data instead.

Does offering security help if my credit is poor?

Generally, yes, and often significantly. Security shifts the lender's risk from "will this business repay based on its history" to "is the asset itself sufficient cover," which is why secured options frequently price well below unsecured ones for the same borrower.

How long does a default stay on my file?

Under Australia's credit reporting rules, a default typically remains on file for 5 years from the date it was listed, regardless of whether it's later paid. However, most lenders weight a paid, aged default (2+ years) far less heavily than a recent or unpaid one.

What's the difference between a default and a court judgment on my file?

A default is a creditor reporting an unpaid debt to a credit bureau. A court judgment means a creditor took the matter to court and obtained an enforceable order against you. Judgments are treated more seriously by lenders because they indicate the dispute went beyond a standard debt recovery process.
