What Is a Business Line of Credit?

A business line of credit is a revolving facility: the lender approves a limit — say $150,000 — and you draw against it whenever you need cash, up to that ceiling. As you repay drawn funds, the limit frees up again for future use, the same way a credit card works but sized and priced for business cash flow rather than everyday spending.

Interest is calculated daily on the drawn balance only, not on the full approved limit. If you draw $40,000 against a $150,000 facility for three weeks and repay it, you pay interest for those three weeks on $40,000 — nothing more. That mechanic is what makes a line of credit suited to irregular or seasonal cash flow: stock purchases, payroll gaps between invoice cycles, or materials costs that spike ahead of a job starting. Most lenders charge a smaller ongoing fee on the undrawn portion of the limit to keep it available, separate from the interest charged on what's actually drawn.

What You Actually Pay: Drawn Balance vs. Full Limit

The gap between a line of credit and a term loan of the same nominal size comes down to what interest is charged against. Worked example: a $200,000 facility, averaging $60,000 drawn across the year, compared with a $200,000 term loan carrying interest on the full balance throughout.

10.5% p.a.

Annual interest: $21,000

$200K LINE OF CREDIT
13.5% p.a.

Despite a higher headline rate, the line of credit costs $11,850 less over the year — because interest only applies to the $60,000 actually in use, not the full $200,000 limit sitting available.

How Much Can You Draw?

Approved limits are sized against the business's ability to service the facility if it were fully drawn, not just the amount requested. Lenders weigh six factors:

Annual Revenue
Unsecured limits typically cap at 8–15% of annual turnover.
Security Offered
Property or asset backing raises the ceiling and improves the rate on the drawn balance.
Time in Business
12+ months trading opens the full panel; under 6 months narrows it to specialist lenders.
Cash Flow Trend
Lenders read 3–6 months of statements for how often the account dips, not just average balance.
Existing Debt Load
Other facilities reduce the serviceable limit a lender will approve.
Credit Score
A clean file broadens the panel; defaults or late payments narrow it to non-bank options.

Real-World Case Study

BRISBANE LANDSCAPING & TRADE

A Brisbane landscaping business with strong summer demand and quiet winter months needed a way to fund materials — turf, pavers, irrigation stock — ahead of each job starting, without carrying a full term loan balance through the slow season. They set up a $180,000 line of credit secured against their work vehicles and equipment. Across the year they drew an average of $52,000, spiking to $140,000 in the pre-summer materials-buying window and sitting near zero through winter. At 12.8% p.a. on the drawn balance plus a 0.6% undrawn fee, their annual cost came to roughly $7,600 — against an estimated $19,600 had they instead carried a $180,000 term loan at a comparable rate for the full year. The saving came directly from only paying for capital while it was actually deployed on materials, not for capacity sitting idle over winter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I pay interest on my full limit or just what I draw?

Just what you draw. Interest is calculated daily on the drawn balance only. Most lenders also charge a smaller fee on the undrawn portion of the limit to keep it available, but this is separate from — and much lower than — the interest rate on funds you've actually used.

Can my limit be reviewed or reduced?

Yes. Most facilities are reviewed annually, and a lender can reduce or decline to renew the limit if trading conditions, revenue, or your credit file have deteriorated since approval. This is different to a term loan, where the approved amount is locked in for the life of the loan.

Is a line of credit better than an overdraft?

It depends on the size and predictability of what you need. A line of credit typically offers a larger limit and is arranged as its own standalone facility, while an overdraft sits on your existing transaction account and suits smaller, occasional buffers. For sizeable, planned working capital needs, a line of credit is usually the better fit.

How is a line of credit different to a credit card?

Structurally similar — both are revolving, both charge interest on the balance used — but a business line of credit is sized for larger working capital needs, typically carries a lower rate than a business credit card, and is assessed against business cash flow rather than a personal spending limit.

Can I use a line of credit for anything?

Most lenders don't restrict use once the facility is approved, though it's intended for working capital — stock, materials, payroll timing, and short-term cash flow gaps — rather than large one-off asset purchases, which are usually better suited to a term loan or equipment finance.

What happens if I don't use the facility?

You'll typically still pay an undrawn-limit fee to keep the facility available, though this is a fraction of the drawn-balance interest rate. Some lenders waive this fee below a minimum utilisation threshold — worth checking when comparing offers.

How often is the facility renewed?

Most business lines of credit are reviewed and renewed annually. The lender reassesses revenue, cash flow, and credit file at each review, and may adjust the limit, rate, or fees based on how the business has performed since the last approval.

Will I need to provide a personal guarantee?

Most lenders require a director's guarantee, regardless of whether the facility is secured against business assets or property. It's a guarantee tied to the director personally, separate from any collateral offered on the facility itself.
