Business Overdraft in Australia: The Complete Guide

Published 5 April 2026 15 min read By Trade Funding

A business overdraft is one of the oldest and most straightforward forms of business finance in Australia. It gives you the ability to spend beyond your account balance when you need to, and automatically resets when money flows back in. For tens of thousands of Australian businesses, an overdraft is the financial safety net that keeps operations running smoothly when cash flow gets tight.

Yet despite its simplicity, many business owners misunderstand how overdrafts work, what they cost, and when they are, or are not, the right solution. This guide covers everything you need to know about business overdrafts in Australia in 2026: how they work, what they cost, who qualifies, and how to decide whether an overdraft is the best option for your business.

What Is a Business Overdraft?

A business overdraft is a flexible credit facility attached directly to your everyday business transaction account. It allows you to withdraw or spend more money than you have in the account, up to a pre-approved limit. When your account balance goes below zero, you are "overdrawn", and you pay interest only on the amount by which your account is overdrawn.

Unlike a term loan, there is no lump sum disbursement. Unlike a line of credit, there is no separate account to manage. The overdraft sits invisibly behind your normal business account, ready to kick in the moment your balance drops below zero.

How a Business Overdraft Works in Practice

Consider a plumbing business with a $50,000 overdraft attached to their NAB business transaction account:

  1. Monday: Account balance is $12,000. Payroll of $28,000 runs, bringing the balance to -$16,000. The overdraft covers the shortfall. Interest starts accruing on $16,000.
  2. Wednesday: A customer pays a $35,000 invoice. The balance goes to +$19,000. Interest stops accruing: the overdrawn amount has been repaid.
  3. Friday: Supplier payments of $25,000 go out. Balance drops to -$6,000. Interest accrues on $6,000.
  4. Following Monday: More customer payments arrive. Balance returns to positive. Interest stops.

Throughout this cycle, the business never had to apply for a loan, wait for approval, or manage a separate facility. The overdraft worked silently in the background, covering gaps as they appeared and resetting as cash flowed back in.

The Key Benefit: Simplicity

A business overdraft is the simplest form of flexible business finance. There is no separate account, no draw-down process, and no fixed repayment schedule. Money flows in and out of your existing account naturally. You pay interest only on the days and amounts you are overdrawn. For small, short-term cash flow gaps, nothing is easier.

Types of Business Overdrafts

Secured vs Unsecured Overdrafts

Secured overdrafts are backed by collateral, most commonly residential or commercial property, but also term deposits, equipment, or a General Security Agreement (GSA) over business assets. Secured facilities offer lower interest rates (from 8% p.a.), higher limits (up to $1 million or more), and greater flexibility at annual review. The downside is that the approval process is longer and your assets are at risk if you default.

Unsecured overdrafts do not require specific collateral, although a personal guarantee from the business directors is almost always required. They are faster to establish and involve less paperwork. However, limits are lower (typically $20,000 to $150,000), rates are higher (10% to 16% p.a.), and lenders are stricter at annual reviews.

Temporary vs Ongoing Overdrafts

Ongoing overdrafts are established with no fixed end date. They continue indefinitely subject to an annual review, where the lender reassesses your business and decides whether to maintain, increase, reduce, or cancel the facility. This is the most common type.

Temporary overdrafts are approved for a specific period, usually 30 to 90 days, to cover a known short-term need. They are often easier to obtain because the lender knows the exposure is time-limited. Common uses include covering a large tax payment, bridging a gap while waiting for a specific debtor payment, or managing a seasonal spike in expenses.

Fee Types to Understand

  • Facility fee (or line fee): Charged on the total approved limit, not just the drawn amount. Typically 0.5% to 1.5% per annum. On a $100,000 overdraft, a 1% facility fee adds $1,000 per year, even if you never use the facility. This is the most commonly overlooked cost.
  • Establishment fee: A one-off charge to set up the overdraft, usually $200 to $600 for smaller facilities or 0.5% to 1% for larger ones.
  • Annual review fee: Some banks charge $200 to $500 at the annual reassessment of your facility.
  • Excess fee: If you exceed your approved limit, banks charge $15 to $50 per occurrence plus penalty interest on the excess amount.
  • Government charges: Stamp duty may apply in some states when establishing or increasing a secured overdraft.
The Hidden Cost: Facility Fees on Unused Limits

Many business owners set up large overdraft facilities "just in case" without realising they are paying a facility fee on the entire limit every year. If you have a $200,000 overdraft but consistently only use $30,000 to $50,000, you are paying the facility fee on $150,000 to $170,000 you never touch. Right-size your facility to your actual usage pattern and save hundreds or thousands per year.

Overdraft Limits: What to Expect

Your overdraft limit depends on several factors:

  • Monthly cash flow: Lenders typically set overdraft limits at 1 to 3 months of average monthly revenue. A business turning over $80,000 per month might qualify for an overdraft of $80,000 to $240,000 depending on other factors.
  • Time in business: Banks generally want 2+ years of trading history for overdraft facilities. Some will consider 12 months for smaller limits.
  • Account conduct: If the overdraft is with your existing bank, they will examine your transaction history closely. Regular deposits, minimal dishonoured payments, and no gambling transactions signal good conduct.
  • Security: Property-secured overdrafts can reach $1 million or more. Unsecured limits are typically capped at $150,000 for the Big 4 banks.
  • Existing debt: Other loans, leases, and credit facilities reduce your available overdraft capacity.

How to Apply for a Business Overdraft

  1. Assess your needs: Review your cash flow patterns over the past 12 months. Identify the maximum gap between expenses and income: this indicates the overdraft limit you need. Do not over-size the facility; you will pay unnecessary fees.
  2. Compare options: Use Trade Funding's free Compare Report to see which lenders match your profile. Even if you already bank with one of the Big 4, you may find a better deal elsewhere.
  3. Approach your existing bank first (if competitive): Your current bank already knows your account conduct and cash flow. If their offer is competitive, this can speed up the process significantly.
  4. Prepare your documents: Have bank statements, BAS, and identification ready before submitting your application.
  5. Submit and wait: Small unsecured overdrafts can be approved within 2 to 5 days with your existing bank. Larger or secured facilities take 10 to 20 days.
  6. Facility establishment: Once approved, the overdraft is linked to your transaction account and available immediately.

Pros and Cons of Business Overdrafts

Advantages

  • Simplest form of flexible business finance, no separate accounts
  • Pay interest only on the overdrawn amount, only for the days overdrawn
  • Instant access, no draw-down process or waiting period
  • Automatic reset as cash flows back into your account
  • No fixed repayment schedule, repays naturally with cash flow
  • Can be combined with other facilities (term loans, cards)
  • Builds relationship and track record with your bank

Disadvantages

  • Facility fees apply even when you do not use the overdraft
  • Annual reviews can result in limit reductions or cancellation
  • Rates are variable, costs can increase without notice
  • Exceeding the limit triggers penalty fees and default interest
  • Lower limits than lines of credit or term loans
  • Must usually bank with the overdraft provider
  • Not suitable for large, one-off funding needs
  • Banks can demand repayment at relatively short notice

When an Overdraft Is the Right Choice

  • You need a small safety net for day-to-day cash flow timing issues
  • Your gaps are short-term (days, not weeks or months)
  • You want the simplest possible facility with no separate accounts to manage
  • Your cash flow is generally healthy but occasionally lumpy
  • You are already banking with the lender and have strong account conduct

When to Choose an Alternative

  • If you need more than $150,000 to $200,000 unsecured, a line of credit offers higher limits
  • If you need a large one-off amount, a term loan is more appropriate
  • If your cash flow problems are caused by slow-paying customers, invoice finance targets the root cause
  • If you need funds for equipment, a chattel mortgage or finance lease offers better rates and tax benefits

Best Use Cases for a Business Overdraft

1. Covering Payroll Timing Gaps

A Sydney-based electrical contracting firm pays 14 staff fortnightly, but most clients pay on 30-day terms. The 2-week gap between payroll and client payments creates a recurring cash shortfall of $25,000 to $40,000. A $50,000 overdraft covers this easily: staff get paid on time, and the overdraft resets when client payments clear.

2. Smoothing Seasonal Expenses

A landscaping business in Brisbane earns 70% of its revenue between September and March. During the quiet winter months, fixed costs like equipment lease payments, insurance, and vehicle registration still need to be paid. A $30,000 overdraft covers these commitments without the business needing to hold excessive cash reserves during peak season.

3. Managing Supplier Payment Terms

A boutique food manufacturer in Melbourne receives a 5% early payment discount from their main ingredient supplier. The discount is worth $15,000 per year but requires payment within 7 days instead of 30. An overdraft allows the business to take the discount consistently, knowing the overdraft will be repaid when their own customers pay within the standard 30-day terms. The $15,000 discount far exceeds the approximately $800 in overdraft interest.

4. Buffering Against Late Payments

A specialist medical practice bills patients through private health funds. Rebates typically arrive within 10 to 14 days but occasionally take 21 to 28 days due to processing delays. A $20,000 overdraft ensures the practice can cover rent, staff wages, and supplies without worrying about the timing of health fund payments.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Using an Overdraft as a Permanent Loan

An overdraft should swing between positive and negative. If your account is permanently overdrawn and you are never repaying the facility, you have a structural cash flow problem, not a timing issue. A term loan with fixed repayments may be more appropriate and almost certainly cheaper.

Banks monitor this closely. If your overdraft is "hard-core", meaning the balance never returns to positive, expect questions at your annual review and a potential facility reduction.

Mistake 2: Over-Sizing the Facility

A $200,000 overdraft sounds reassuring, but if you typically only need $30,000 to $60,000, you are paying a facility fee on $140,000 to $170,000 you never use. Right-size your facility based on your actual cash flow patterns from the past 12 months, plus a reasonable buffer of 20% to 30%.

Mistake 3: Not Preparing for the Annual Review

Banks review overdraft facilities every 12 months. If your business performance has declined, your account conduct has deteriorated, or you cannot provide current financial statements, the bank may reduce or cancel your facility, often with just 30 to 90 days notice. Prepare for your review by having updated financials ready, ensuring your account is in good order, and proactively addressing any issues.

Mistake 4: Ignoring the Total Cost

Overdraft interest rates look reasonable (8% to 16% p.a.), but the total cost includes facility fees, review fees, and any excess charges. A $100,000 overdraft at 11% with a 1% facility fee costs $12,000 per year in interest and fees if the facility is fully drawn for the entire year. If you are consistently at maximum, compare this against a term loan which might be cheaper.

Mistake 5: Not Comparing Providers

Many businesses set up their overdraft with their existing bank without checking alternatives. Rates, fees, and limits vary significantly between lenders. A 2% difference in rate on a $100,000 facility saves $2,000 per year. Over 5 years, that is $10,000, worth the effort of comparing.
